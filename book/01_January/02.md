## January 2nd - Overcome Impostor Syndrome

***“What, then, is the fruit of these doctrines? Precisely that which must needs be both the fairest and the most becoming for those who are being truly educated—tranquillity, fearlessness, freedom. For on these matters we should not trust the multitude, who say, ‘Only the free can be educated,’ but rather the philosophers, who say, ‘Only the educated are free.’”***
—EPICTETUS, DISCOURSES, 2.1.21–23

You’re in a design review with people who have been doing this for fifteen years. Someone uses an acronym you don’t know, and you nod. Afterwards you look it up and feel like a fraud for nodding. On the train home you scroll past a former classmate’s post about their new staff title, and the feeling gets worse.

Most developers know this voice. It says the real engineers are somewhere else: the ones with the right degree, the right company on their profile, the side project with ten thousand stars. Epictetus heard a version of it in his own time. People said only the free could be educated, meaning the freeborn. He had been a slave, and he answered that it works the other way round. Education is what makes you free.

He didn’t mean diplomas. He meant learning to judge things correctly, starting with what is yours to worry about and what isn’t. The title someone else got belongs to them. The acronym you didn’t know was a five-minute search. Your attention to the work and your honesty about what you don’t understand yet belong to you, and nobody in that review can take them away.

That is the kind of education this book is about. You’ll keep learning frameworks too, but the calm Epictetus promises comes from seeing your own situation clearly. It grows a little every time you practice it, and you can practice it every day.
