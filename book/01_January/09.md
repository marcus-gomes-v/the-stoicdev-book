## January 9th - Navigating Control in Professional Challenges

***“Some things are under our control, while others are not under our control. Under our control are conception, choice, desire, aversion, and, in a word, everything that is our own doing; not under our control are our body, our property, reputation, office, and, in a word, everything that is not our own doing. Furthermore, the things under our control are by nature free, unhindered, and unimpeded; while the things not under our control are weak, servile, subject to hindrance, and not our own.”***
—EPICTETUS, ENCHIRIDION, 1.1-2

These are the first lines of the Enchiridion, the short handbook of Epictetus’ teaching that his student Arrian put together. The name means something you keep at hand, and it opens with a sorting exercise. A good part of Stoicism is simply practice at doing that sorting well.

Try it on a bad week. A teammate promised the API would be ready on Monday, and it’s Thursday. Another one keeps presenting shared work at the all-hands as if it were only theirs. Your manager hasn’t mentioned the promotion conversation since March.

Now sort. Your teammate’s delivery date isn’t yours. Neither is the other one’s need to be seen, nor your manager’s memory. Reputation and office, Epictetus says, go in the other pile. Someone else can block them at any moment, which is exactly why they feel so heavy when you try to carry them.

What’s left is smaller, and it’s free. Your judgment about what happened: maybe the API is late because the spec changed twice. Your choice of what to say: a short, direct message to the person, instead of a sarcastic comment in the team channel. What you want, and what you’re trying to avoid, both of which you can adjust. The breath you take before replying, or the walk around the block, is when the sorting happens. Nobody can stop you from doing it, and once things are in the right pile, the next step is usually obvious.
