## February 13th - The Illusion of Indulgence

***“When you get an external impression of some pleasure, guard yourself, as with impressions in general, against being carried away by it; nay, let the matter wait upon your leisure, and give yourself a little delay. Next think of the two periods of time, first, that in which you will enjoy your pleasure, and second, that in which, after the enjoyment is over, you will later repent and revile your own self; and set over against these two periods of time how much joy and self-satisfaction you will get if you refrain. However, if you feel that a suitable occasion has arisen to do the deed, be careful not to allow its enticement, and sweetness, and attractiveness to overcome you; but set over against all this the thought, how much better is the consciousness of having won a victory over it.”***
—EPICTETUS, ENCHIRIDION, 34

It’s six in the evening and the feature is almost done. You could write the tests now, or you could merge it, move the ticket to done, and go home feeling great. The tests can come later. Everyone knows how that ends.

Epictetus gives a method for moments like this, and it’s surprisingly practical. Don’t act on the impression straight away. Give it a little delay. Then picture two moments: the next ten minutes, when skipping the tests feels wonderful, and the week after, when the bug shows up in production and you’re explaining to your lead why there was no test. Set against both the quiet satisfaction of having done it properly.

It works for every pleasant shortcut: the hour of scrolling before you start the hard task, the shiny library you want to add because it looks fun, the quick hack instead of the boring migration. He doesn’t say pleasure is always wrong. If the moment is right, take it. Just don’t let its sweetness make the decision for you, and notice how good it feels to win that small fight with yourself.
