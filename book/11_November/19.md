## November 19th - TRUST THE PATH

***“Upon every occasion we ought to have the following thoughts at our command:
Lead thou me on, O Zeus, and Destiny,
To that goal long ago to me assigned.
I’ll follow and not falter; if my will
Prove weak and craven, still I’ll follow on.
‘Whoso has rightly with necessity complied,
We count him wise, and skilled in things divine.’
‘Well, O Crito, if so it is pleasing to the gods, so let it be.’
‘Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but they cannot hurt me.’”***
—EPICTETUS, ENCHIRIDION, 53

Epictetus closes his handbook with these lines, and almost none of them are his. The verse is by Cleanthes, who as a young man drew water in gardens at night so he could study philosophy by day. Then come two lines of Euripides, and two sentences of Socrates, one from his prison cell and one from his trial. Epictetus wanted them ready for any occasion.

What they share is a refusal to fight what is already settled. Cleanthes even admits his will might prove weak and craven, and says he’ll follow anyway.

That admission is the useful part. You won’t always feel ready. The 2 a.m. page doesn’t wait for you to feel brave. The project you didn’t want lands on your plate, and the meeting where you explain a failure arrives on schedule. You can walk in reluctant. You just have to walk in.

Keep the final line close: they can kill me, but they cannot hurt me. A bad quarter can cost you a bonus. It can’t reach the part of you that decides how to behave.
